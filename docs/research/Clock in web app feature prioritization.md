# Ship a trustworthy punch ledger first

Build the MVP around one promise, that every punch is recorded correctly, once, and can be explained later, and defer everything that is expensive, legally risky or demanded only by larger customers. The recommended MVP is web/PWA clock in/out with a shared-kiosk PIN option, break tracking, timesheets with manager edit and approval, one hard-coded overtime policy, CSV export, role-based access, an append-only audit log, and an idempotent offline queue. This is roughly **10-16 engineer-weeks** for a 1-3 person TypeScript/Postgres team (my estimate, not a published benchmark; the one external anchor is a vendor anecdote of about 4.5 months and $36,000 for a simple custom build ([TimeTrex](https://www.timetrex.com/blog/analysis-of-time-and-attendance-software))). Scheduling, leave accruals, geofencing, photos and a full PWA offline layer add about 30-45 engineer-weeks, and payroll integrations, SSO, biometrics and a configurable rules engine push toward a year. The decisive finding is that the features that are hardest to retrofit are not the visible ones: immutable events, UTC plus IANA zones, tenant isolation, audit logging, versioned pay rules and retention fields must exist on day one, while geofencing, photos, kiosks, SSO and notifications can be added later with modest rework. Buyers reward reliability over breadth, and basic clocking is already free at the low end, so the MVP must be reliable rather than large. Evidence caveat: no independent feature-importance survey exists, most sources are vendor pages, and all effort sizes and the Kano-style classification are inference.

## Reliability, not breadth, drives purchase and retention

No formal survey with sample sizes quantifies which time-and-attendance features matter most, so importance has to be triangulated. G2 says automated overtime calculation and payroll integration stand out as the most valued features ([G2](https://www.g2.com/categories/time-attendance)), and Capterra lists clock in/out, timesheet management, payroll, reporting, mobile access and scheduling ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/attendance-tracking-software/buyers-guide/)). One vendor argues buyers wrongly shop by feature count and should start from the payroll edge cases they cannot tolerate, which makes pay-calculation correctness the real purchase driver ([Asure](https://www.asuresoftware.com/why-most-time-and-attendance-buying-decisions-start-in-the-wrong-place/)). Complaints point the same direction. Connecteam reviewers report a time clock that "freezes" and forgets clock state ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/p/153140/Connecteam/reviews/?page=9)); Buddy Punch drew complaints of inaccurate mobile recording, recurring GPS errors, corrections that require manager intervention, no native offline mode and privacy worries about photos and location ([Jibble](https://www.jibble.io/reviews/buddy-punch/5-ways-buddy-punch-sucks); [CheckThat.ai](https://checkthat.ai/brands/buddy-punch/reviews)). Many of these come from competitor-authored pages, so they are hypotheses, but the pattern is consistent: retention depends on "it records time correctly every time," easy corrections and correct overtime, while price and ease of use win the sale.

The free tiers of incumbents set the floor an MVP must clear. Homebase's free plan covers up to 10 employees at one location with basic time tracking, with location-based clock-in, photo capture and custom overtime and break settings starting at its $30-per-location Essentials tier ([Homebase](https://www.joinhomebase.com/pricing)). Jibble's free plan includes unlimited users, offline mode, reminders, GPS and limited face recognition, with overtime, more geofences and leave tracking in paid tiers ([Jibble](https://www.jibble.io/upgrade-plans)). Clockify and Toggl gate approvals, invoicing and richer reporting behind paid plans ([Clockify](https://clockify.me/learn/resources/paid-features-explained/); [Toggl](https://support.toggl.com/payment-plans-at-toggl-track)). Reading across them, capture, timesheets and basic export are free-tier territory, while location or photo verification, approvals, overtime and break rules, long retention, integrations and security are what vendors monetize. Because Jibble bundles GPS and face recognition at no cost, an entrant cannot treat those as premium differentiators; the earlier feature-landscape research reached the same conclusion, that differentiation shifts to jurisdiction-aware rules, reliable offline capture, privacy-safe identity checks and honest pricing.

A proposed Kano classification, which is my hypothesis and should be validated with 15-30 users per segment, sorts features accordingly. Must-be features, whose absence causes dissatisfaction, are clock in/out, accurate timestamps, timesheets, manager edit and approval with an audit trail, CSV export, overtime and break calculation, reliable offline capture and access control. Performance features, where more is better, are kiosk and PIN speed, geofence accuracy, scheduling and swaps, leave, reports and payroll integrations. Delighters are explainable anomaly flags, auto clock-in on zone entry, Slack or Teams punching and employee-facing earnings estimates. Some features are reverse-Kano: frequent photo prompts, always-on GPS and forced biometrics generate privacy complaints and legal exposure. Frameworks fit in sequence: MoSCoW to cut MVP scope, Kano to classify, and RICE (Reach x Impact x Confidence / Effort) only inside tiers of a large backlog ([Plane](https://plane.so/blog/feature-prioritization-frameworks-rice-moscow-and-kano-explained); [Lovable](https://lovable.dev/guides/rice-moscow-kano)). Segment needs also differ, which argues for one beachhead: shift/hourly teams need kiosk, breaks, overtime, scheduling and offline, field crews need offline, GPS and job tagging, and office or contractor users want timers, leave and project billing. Shift and field share the clock, timesheet, offline and overtime core ([ClockShark](https://www.clockshark.com/tour/gps-time-clock); [Homebase](https://www.joinhomebase.com/pricing)). Segment evidence is thin and vendor-sourced, and office/hybrid evidence is essentially absent.

## The ranked matrix: importance against effort

The matrix below combines the importance evidence above with effort estimates for a production-quality first version, including tests, on a managed-auth, Postgres, TypeScript baseline. Sizes are S = 2-5 days, M = 1-3 weeks, L = 3-6 weeks, XL = 6+ weeks. All sizes are my judgement; no published engineer-day benchmarks for these features were found. "Hidden cost" flags features whose hard parts are invisible in a demo.

| 3 | Audit log and reason-required corrections | Must-be | M (1-2 weeks if designed in; L if retrofitted) | Medium | MVP |

| 22 | Anomaly detection, Slack/Teams punching | Delighter | M-L | Medium | v2 |

The ranking logic is a value-over-effort sort adjusted for dependency and risk. Items 1 to 9 combine must-be importance with low-to-moderate effort or are prerequisites for everything else, so they ship together. Item 8 is the stretch case: offline is a must-be (it is a recurring review complaint and a core field-crew need) but is deceptively hard, so the MVP ships only a punch queue, which is L at 2-3 weeks, rather than full sync, which is XL.

Several estimates rest on platform facts worth knowing. Safari on iOS does not support Background Sync, so queued punches only transmit when the app is reopened ([caniuse](https://caniuse.com/wf-background-sync)), and Web Push on iOS works only for apps installed to the home screen ([Braze](https://www.braze.com/resources/articles/mobile-web-push-is-now-supported-on-safari)). Both argue for an app-level outbox that flushes on open and on the online event, rather than relying on service-worker sync. Browser geolocation is agnostic to its source and carries no guarantee of the true location ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/TR/2025/REC-geolocation-20250818/)), and DevTools can override it entirely ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/microsoft-edge/devtools/sensors)), so geofencing is a soft signal that costs M effort but cannot be made tamper-proof. Managed services cut effort where they exist: SSO via a provider such as WorkOS is reported at about $125 per connection per month for SSO and separately for directory sync, a third-party figure to verify ([SSOJet](https://ssojet.com/blog/workos-pricing-at-scale-50-100-200-connections)), and AWS Rekognition Face Liveness is billed per check, reportedly including failed attempts ([AWS re:Post](https://repost.aws/questions/QUvjRd9nCgS66oQ_Vv1rLJUg/how-does-amazon-rekognition-liveness-charges-users-per-successful-or-unsuccessful-attempt-or-both)). Nothing off the shelf solves the pay-rules engine or leave accruals, which makes them the best candidates for aggressive descoping.

## MVP and later phases: what ships when and what waits

The MVP should be small enough to ship in roughly three to four months but complete enough to be used for real payroll. It contains web/PWA clock in/out, an optional shared-kiosk PIN, break start/stop, a timesheet view, manager edits that require a reason, approval and period locking, one configurable overtime rule, CSV export, three roles, a punch-only offline queue with idempotent sync, a basic daily and weekly hours report, and email alerts for missed punches and approvals. This is consistent with dev-agency phasing for time tracking, which puts capture, approved timesheets, reporting and cross-device sync in phase one, integrations, billing, mobile and context capture such as GPS in phase two, and analytics, role-based access, audit logs and GDPR controls in phase three ([8ration](https://www.8ration.com/blogs/time-tracking-software-development/)). The attendance-specific adjustment is to pull audit logs and access control forward into the MVP, because for hourly payroll they are not enterprise extras, and to add offline and kiosk, which a project-time-tracking source underweights. That source also quotes 3-5 months and $25K-$45K for an MVP, a vendor figure for project time tracking that is an order-of-magnitude reference only.

Version 1.x (about 6-9 further months of small-team work) adds the features that reduce friction or open a second customer type. Geolocation stamps with a flag-and-review geofence, non-biometric selfie capture, a live dashboard, web push, and tenant-level rounding settings come first, as they are cheap if geo and media references are already on each punch. Scheduling with swaps and leave with accruals follow, each a 4-8 week build, and they should be sequenced by beachhead: shift teams want scheduling first, office and field users want leave. The first one or two payroll integrations come after CSV export has proven which vendors customers use, since employee and earning-code mapping and partner-program timelines, not API calls, dominate the cost. Where possible, start with CSV in the vendor's import format, then a unified-API provider, then direct connections.

Version 2 and on-demand items are those where legal, scale or sales pressure justifies cost. A configurable multi-jurisdiction rules engine should be preceded by code-defined per-tenant policies with a fixtures table of test cases; the earlier research showed real demand for premium-pay automation (for example California's one-hour premium per missed meal or rest break ([SHRM](https://www.shrm.org/in/topics-tools/employment-law-compliance/navigating-californias-meal-rest-break-requirements))), but a UI rule builder invites scope creep. SSO and SCIM wait for the first customer who asks, preferably through an auth provider with organization support chosen at the start. Face verification should come last and only with consent capture, retention controls and a non-biometric fallback; photo plus manager review is the recommended stepping stone. Anomaly detection is credible only as explainable flags that start a review, not as proof of fraud ([Truein](https://truein.com/blogs/ai-anomaly-detection-time-tracking)). SOC 2 should be adopted as controls early (access reviews, logging, backups, change management) and audited only when deals require it; Type 1 is quoted at roughly 3-8 months and $5k-$60k and Type 2 at $12k-$100k with a 3-12 month observation window, all vendor estimates ([Drata](https://drata.com/learn/soc-2/type-1-vs-type-2); [Scrut](https://www.scrut.io/soc-2/cost-of-soc-2-audit)).

## Day-one design decisions that cannot be bolted on later

The central design principle is that raw punches are immutable facts and everything else is derived. Retrofitting an insert-only model onto a mutable timesheet row loses history that was already overwritten and cannot be reconstructed, which is why this ranks first. The punch table should hold event types (clock in, clock out, break start, break end, void), a client-generated UUID as idempotency key, employee, tenant and location, source (web, kiosk, offline, manual), the device-claimed time and the server-received time as separate fields, device or kiosk id, optional geo with accuracy, an optional photo reference and flags. Corrections are new adjustment events referencing the original, with actor, reason, before and after, and approver; the application's database role should hold no UPDATE or DELETE privilege on that table. Rounded and overtime totals are computed "pay segments" stored with the version of the rule that produced them. Keeping raw punches is also a legal hedge: the FLSA permits rounding to the nearest 5 minutes, tenth or quarter hour only if it averages out neutrally ([Cornell LII, 29 CFR 785.48](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/29/785.48)), courts examine how rounding is applied, not only how it is written (Corbin v. Time Warner was upheld ([Ogletree](https://ogletree.com/insights/ninth-circuit-approves-neutral-time-clock-rounding-practice))), and a 2026 DOL opinion letter found a hospital's rounding of early clock-ins but not early clock-outs to be non-neutral ([DOL](https://www.dol.gov/sites/dolgov/files/WHD/opinion-letters/FLSA/FLSA2026-8.pdf)).

Time handling is the second foundation. Store every instant as a UTC timestamp, store each location's IANA zone name rather than an offset, and compute the local work date and pay period at write time with the zone in effect, so later rule changes do not rewrite history. Store scheduled shifts as local time plus zone. A shift across a fall-back change can show a clock-out earlier than the clock-in in naive local time ([Data-Basics](https://blog.data-basics.com/daylight-saving-time-creates-a-mini-y2k-crisis-twice-a-year)), and recurrence libraries have had bugs that shift or duplicate instances across DST ([rrule issue 300](https://www.github.com/jakubroztocil/rrule/issues/300)). The workweek start day and time must be a per-tenant setting, since the FLSA requires employers to record when the workweek begins and daily and weekly hours ([US DOL Fact Sheet 21](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/21-flsa-recordkeeping)). Native Temporal support is not yet in stable Safari, so use Luxon, date-fns-tz or a polyfill ([caniuse](https://caniuse.com/temporal)). Use integer minutes or fixed-precision decimals, never floats, for hours and money.

Tenancy and authorization are third. Put tenant_id, and location_id where relevant, on every table with composite foreign keys, and enforce Postgres row-level security as a safety net over application filters. AWS guidance calls RLS required for tenant isolation in a pooled model ([AWS Prescriptive Guidance](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/prescriptive-guidance/latest/saas-multitenant-managed-postgresql/rls.html)); known pitfalls are that table owners bypass policies unless FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY is set, and session-level tenant context can leak across pooled connections, so use transaction-local settings ([AWS Database Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/database/multi-tenant-data-isolation-with-postgresql-row-level-security)). In a punch and timesheet API, the likeliest vulnerabilities are broken object-level and function-level authorization, API1 and API5 in the OWASP API Top 10, and punch endpoints are "sensitive business flows" needing rate limits ([OWASP](https://api-security.owasp.org/editions/2023/en/0x11-t10)). Roles are scoped (manager of which locations), which is harder than a role column. Also separate employee from login user so kiosk-only staff exist, support one person in several tenants, and soft-delete terminated employees to preserve history.

The remaining day-one items are cheaper but equally hard to add late. Pay-period locking and effective-dated, versioned rules make past periods reproducible. A ledger for leave balances (append-only accrual, usage and adjustment transactions, balance as a sum) beats a mutable balance column once accruals arrive. Postgres exclusion constraints on tstzrange prevent overlapping shifts or punch intervals in the database ([Supabase](https://supabase.com/blog/range-columns.md)). An outbox table and idempotency keys underpin webhooks, notifications and integrations. Retention must be per-tenant and per-record-class configurable with a legal-hold flag, because minimums differ: the US requires 3 years for payroll records and 2 years for time cards, producible within 72 hours ([eCFR Part 516](https://www.ecfr.gov/api/renderer/v1/content/enhanced/current/title-29?part=516)), the EU requires an "objective, reliable and accessible" daily time-recording system ([CJEU press release](https://curia.europa.eu/jcms/upload/docs/application/pdf/2019-05/cp190061en.pdf)), and other jurisdictions such as Israel and the UK have conflicting or unverified figures in the sources found. Add consent and notice records and a tenant switch to disable location entirely. Location should be captured at the moment of punch only, and coarse (inside fence yes or no) by default, which matches guidance that tracking be limited to working hours and that precise location is hard to justify where an entry log suffices ([Law Society of Ireland Gazette](https://lawsociety.ie/gazette/top-stories/2026/march/hard-limits-on-a-bosss-right-to-track-workers); [ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/advice-for-small-organisations/whats-new/blogs/employee-monitoring-is-it-right-for-your-business/)). By contrast, reports, dashboards, notification UI, SSO (with org-aware auth), kiosks, geofence rules and photo capture are cheap to add later if geo evidence and media references already exist on punches.

## Key risks and how the ranking mitigates them

The largest risks are correctness and legal ones rather than technical novelty. Pay-rule error is the first: rounding, overtime and break logic that drifts from legal requirements creates wage-claim exposure, and the pay engine is the single most deceptive build at 6-12+ weeks for a configurable one. The mitigation is to ship one hard-coded policy per tenant with a fixtures-based test table, keep raw punches, make computation deterministic and re-runnable after retroactive edits, and avoid a UI rule builder until demand proves its shape. Omission risk is just as real: wrongly computed or untraceable pay, a cross-tenant leak, or regulatory action on location or biometrics. The 2021 ransomware attack on Kronos took client timekeeping offline for weeks, and workers sued over paychecks estimated from prior hours ([TechTarget](https://www.techtarget.com/enterprise-software/news/252512253/Tesla-PepsiCo-workers-bring-lawsuit-over-UKG-payroll-outage)), which shows that payroll-critical software must treat backups with tested restores and exportable data as MVP concerns.

Biometric and location privacy is the sharpest legal risk. Illinois BIPA litigation remains active, with a class certification upheld against ITS in 2025 and Speedway settling for $12.1M ([IDTechWire](https://idtechwire.com/illinois-appeals-court-upholds-class-action-in-biometric-privacy-suit-against-its-technologies/); [Mealey's](https://www.mealeys.com/mealeys/articles/2377545)). The 2024 amendment limits recovery to one per person for repeated collection, but exposure stays material ([Baker Donelson](https://www.bakerdonelson.com/bipa-amendments-passed-by-illinois-legislature-what-companies-need-to-know)). Primary BIPA text, Texas and Washington laws and Israeli retention rules were not verified, so obtain legal review before any face feature or non-US launch. This is why the recommended order is photo with manager review, then biometrics only on request. Fraud controls carry a softer risk: because browser location and device clocks are user-controlled, treat them as evidence plus flags (impossible travel, duplicate device, IP mismatch, large clock skew) and prefer flag-and-review to blocking. Buddy punching is better addressed by per-user credentials, device binding and photo-on-punch where lawful than by biometrics, and no independent source quantifies buddy-punching losses or spoof-detection accuracy.

Delivery risks cluster in four places. Offline is the biggest scope trap: conflict policies vary across products (newest edit wins, online entry wins with the offline one flagged, per-day user choice), and last-write-wins silently discards edits ([timesheet.io](https://timesheet.io/en/blog/offline-time-tracking-sync); [Jibble](https://www.jibble.io/help/how-to-track-time-without-an-internet-connection)), so for punches use append-only events with flag-and-review and record both device and server time. iOS storage eviction and stale service workers are additional hazards. Scheduling and leave are deceptively large because of DST recurrence, swap state machines, accrual caps, carryover and backdating. Integrations are gated by vendor approval programs and mapping, not code. Burst load at shift start is manageable: keep the punch endpoint a tiny idempotent insert, move derivation to async workers, and add client retry jitter; a managed Postgres with a queue should suffice early, though this sizing is unsourced reasoning and no vendor load data was found. Finally, trying to serve every segment at once is a strategic risk; picking one beachhead, shift or field, concentrates the must-be list.

## Conclusion

Seen together, the evidence reverses a common instinct: the roadmap's hardest decisions sit in the schema, not the feature list. Almost every high-value feature, from geofence flags to premium-pay automation to audit-ready exports, becomes a modest addition once immutable events, zone-aware dates, tenant isolation and versioned rules exist, and an expensive migration, or an unrecoverable loss of history, if they do not. A good plan therefore front-loads about 3-4 weeks of foundations that no customer will see, and in exchange makes later phases additive.

The research also leaves the highest-stakes questions open. No independent source ranks feature importance by segment, no benchmark validates the effort estimates, and legal details for biometrics, non-US retention and rounding were not verified. Closing them before committing to v1.x scope means interviewing 15-30 users in the chosen beachhead segment, prototyping the offline queue and the overtime policy against real payroll samples to calibrate effort, and getting a short legal review on the retention defaults, any location capture and any face-verification feature.
